The rule

Count 1 is the white spot 3. Each jump: multiply by 3, then wrap around 31.

Everyone knows the start spot and the rule. They are public.

I secretly pick a count of 7.
After 7 counts I land on spot 17.

Public key: 17

Forward is easy

Knowing 7, finding 17 is quick. Computers have shortcuts (double, double, add), so even a 116-digit count takes well under a millisecond.

Backward is hard

Someone sees only 17. What was the count? The spots look random, so the only way is to try:

eMudhra failed at job 3: the keys were not created correctly.

Key ceremony = a scripted, filmed, audited event where a CA creates new signing keys. Every step is written down in advance.
HSM = a locked hardware box. Keys are supposed to be born inside it from good randomness and never leave it in readable form.
30 April 2025: eMudhra creates a batch of 24 new intermediate CA keys under its emSign ECC Root CA - G3 hierarchy.
Mid-2025: the hierarchy is trusted by browsers. In May 2025 Sectigo cross-signs eight of the intermediates. Two yearly audits pass.
Eight of these keys were broken from the moment they were made.
eMudhra's own explanation, still under investigation. A test of key wrapping created a second, weak key pair with a similar label. Staff used the weak one to make the certificate requests. Human errors at multiple steps.

The safety net. Certificate Transparency: public, append-only logs. Major browsers reject a certificate that is not logged. A forged certificate must either be logged, where the world can see it, or be rejected. Nothing was found in the logs, so browsers were very likely safe. Other software usually does not check logs.

The rule

Every new website certificate must be written into public logs. The log gives a receipt, which goes inside the certificate. Chrome, Safari, Edge and Firefox reject certificates without receipts.

Forged and logged?

Everyone can see it. Monitors would flag a bank.com certificate from an eMudhra CA within hours.

Forged, not logged?

The browser refuses it. The attack fails.
Logs show no certificates from these eight intermediates in the whole 18 months. Browsers were very likely never attacked.
But curl, Python, Java, Go programs, many phone apps and IoT devices do not check CT logs. For them, nobody can be sure.
The keys were effectively public from day one, for about seventeen and a half months. There was a public clue: revocation lists signed by these CAs began failing checks in May 2025.

The cleanup

How do you un-trust a key that is already inside billions of devices?

Classic revocation: the CA publishes a cancelled list (CRL) or answers live questions (OCSP). The weakness: if the answer does not come, most software carries on. An attacker in the middle can block the question. And many apps never ask.

eMudhra revoked the eight intermediates and listed them in its cancelled list (CRL).

Software can also ask the CA live (OCSP): "is this certificate still good?"

But an attacker in the middle can simply block the question.

Most software "fails open": no answer means carry on. And many apps never ask at all.

So browser makers built their own block lists, pushed straight into the browser.

Each browser has a push channel for emergencies. Chrome's CRLSets were live in under two hours. Firefox's OneCRL the same evening. Apple and Microsoft within three days. Then the root itself was removed.

What should you do?

  

    

      
Everyone
  • Update your browser and restart it.
  • Install phone and computer system updates.
  • Treat any certificate warning as a real warning.

      
Developers
  • Update runtimes and CA bundles: certifi, Node.js, Go, Java, Rust crates.
  • Rebuild and redeploy container images.
  • Check indirect dependencies too.
  • Never "fix" new errors with verify=False, curl -k or InsecureSkipVerify.

  • Certificates under emSign ECC Root CA - G3? Replace them before 27 Oct.
  • Update trust stores in proxies, mutual-TLS setups and appliances.
  • Watch CT logs for certificates issued for your own domains.

    

  







  
Five lessons. The last is the hopeful one: the system's openness let a stranger find the flaw and let the whole ecosystem fix it in days.

  
Lessons

  
Five things to take home

  

    
1Trust on the web is shared.Any one trusted CA can vouch for any website, so the weakest CA sets the level for everyone.

    
2A key is only as strong as the way it was chosen.P-384 is excellent maths. A private key of 4 makes it worthless.

    
3Certified hardware and audits are not proof.The process around them failed, and two audit cycles did not catch it.

    
4Openness works.Public certificates and public logs let one outsider find the flaw, and showed browsers were very likely never attacked.

    
5Trust lists must update fast.Browsers fixed it in hours. Everything else is still catching up.
